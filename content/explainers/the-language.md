Say the word "pork" and you picture a plate. Say "pig" and you picture an animal. English has a whole vocabulary that points at the food and away from the creature: pork, beef, veal, mutton, poultry. Add the vocabulary of the industry itself (processing, product, throughput, harvest), and a pig can go from a being to a commodity without anyone telling a lie.

This is mostly culture and habit. Words drift this way in many areas of life, and nobody now chooses to say "beef". The question worth asking is whether it makes any difference. There is some evidence that it does.

## What the experiments found

Jonas Kunst and Sigrid Hohle ran five studies in Norway and the US with more than a thousand participants, mostly meat eaters. [Summarising their findings](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2016/10/161011125655.htm), the University of Oslo reports:

- **Processing.** Participants saw chicken as a whole bird, drumsticks, or chopped fillets. The more processed it was, the less they linked it to an animal and the less empathy they felt.
- **Presentation.** Shown roast pork with or without its head, participants felt less empathy and disgust about the beheaded version, and were less willing to choose a vegetarian alternative.
- **Menus.** Menus listing "pig" and "cow" in place of "pork" and "beef" made people less willing to eat the meat, and changed their empathy and disgust.
- **Verbs.** A related test found that "harvest" instead of "slaughtered" or "killed" reduced empathy for the animal, even though harvest ordinarily refers to plants.

The authors call the idea the disassociation hypothesis: presentation lets people avoid the thought that meat was an animal. This summary is a press report of the paper rather than the paper itself, and these were experiments on stated reactions, not on what people actually ate over weeks.

## The mind behind the meat

A second line of research looks at what we think animals are like. Brock Bastian and colleagues found in 2012 that [animals regarded as suitable to eat were rated as having lower mental capacities](https://www.research.ed.ac.uk/en/publications/dont-mind-meat-the-denial-of-mind-to-animals-used-for-human-consu/); meat eaters denied minds to food animals more when reminded of the link between meat and animal suffering; and expecting to eat meat soon increased mind denial, which in turn reduced negative feelings. We come back to this in [Why we love dogs and eat pigs](/why/dogs-and-pigs/).

Taken together, these suggest a loop. Words and packaging make it easier not to think of the animal, and not thinking of the animal makes it easier to eat.

## Where this does and does not point

It would be a mistake to say vocabulary alone explains anything. People who know perfectly well where beef comes from still say "beef". And "pork" is not deceptive in any ordinary sense.

The narrower and safer claim is that wording is one of the things that makes the animal easy to forget. If you suspect that is true for you, there is a simple test: for a day, say the animal's name. Notice what changes, if anything.

If an argument you have heard leans on the animals not really minding, [animals not conscious](/a/animals-not-conscious/) looks at what the evidence says about that.

## In short

- English separates meat words (pork, beef) from animal words (pig, cow); industry terms add more distance.
- In experiments, plainer wording, visible heads and less processing raised empathy and disgust; the reverse lowered them.
- People also tend to rate animals they eat as having less mind, especially when reminded of suffering.
- This is habit, not conspiracy, and the evidence is experimental, not proof about everyday eating.
