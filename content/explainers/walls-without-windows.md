Most people have never seen the inside of a piggery, a hatchery or an abattoir. That is not because they looked away. It is because nobody can wander in. A farm is private property, which is a perfectly ordinary rule, and the animals' lives are lived behind it.

This is not unique to farming, and it is not necessarily sinister. Factories, labs and hospitals are not open to the public either. But when the thing hidden is where our food comes from, and when the law is then used to make recording or publicising it a crime, it is fair to ask what that does to how much any of us know.

## What the laws say

In the US, critics call these measures "ag-gag" laws. Idaho's is a clear example. According to the Animal Legal Defense Fund, which was a party to the case, [an undercover investigator filmed dairy workers in 2012, and lawmakers then passed a law criminalising undercover agricultural investigations](https://aldf.org/case/challenging-idahos-ag-gag-law/). A district court struck it down in 2015. In 2018 the Ninth Circuit held that the ban on recording violated the First Amendment, but upheld two narrowed provisions about misrepresentation to obtain records or a job with intent to cause economic harm.

The courts are not unanimous. In Iowa, the Eighth Circuit in 2021 [held that a provision on false statements in job applications was overbroad, but reversed the ruling against the provision on gaining access under false pretences](https://www.rcfp.org/briefs-comments/animal-legal-defense-fund-v-reynolds/). So the position in the US is mixed: some of these laws have fallen, some stand.

## Australia

In 2019 the federal parliament passed the Criminal Code Amendment (Agricultural Protection) Act. As [described by The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/it-isnt-clear-how-the-new-bill-against-animal-rights-activists-will-protect-farmers-120588), the bill made it an offence to use a carriage service, such as social media, to distribute material intended to incite trespass on agricultural land (up to 12 months' imprisonment), or to incite unlawful damage or theft there (up to five years). It exempts public-interest reporting by a professional journalist. Agricultural land, in the bill, includes piggeries, chicken farms, abattoirs and saleyards.

The Law Council of Australia [argued the bill overlapped with state and territory law and could stifle legitimate public debate](https://lawcouncil.au/media/media-releases/get-the-balance-right-on-farm-trespass-laws-says-law-council), and that its journalist and whistleblower protections might be inadequate. The government's reply, in the words reported there, was that trespass and property damage remain subject to state and territory law.

Farmers had a real grievance behind these laws, and it deserves to be stated fairly. Activists entering farms can disturb animals, spread disease and frighten families, and many farmers feel targeted. In NSW, the Right to Farm Act of 2019 [raised the maximum penalty for aggravated farm trespass to a $22,000 fine and three years in prison](https://www.nswnationals.org.au/historic-legislation-to-protect-farmers-passed/), according to a release from the party that promoted it.

## What the effect is

Two things can be true at once. The laws respond to genuine trespass concerns, and they also make it harder for the public to see inside the industry. A reader who has never been inside a shed, and is not likely to be shown one, will form their picture from what is available: packaging, advertising, and the [storybook farm](/why/storybook-farm/).

This matters for the debate on this site too. When someone says the animals have good lives, the honest reply is that most of us are not in a position to check, which is why independent records matter. For the argument that welfare labels settle it, see [humane meat](/a/humane-meat/).

## In short

- Farms are private, and some jurisdictions have added laws against filming, misrepresentation to gain entry, or inciting trespass.
- Courts in the US have split: Idaho's recording ban fell in 2018, while Iowa's access-by-false-pretences provision survived in 2021.
- Australia's 2019 federal law targets online incitement; the Law Council warned it could chill public debate. NSW raised its maximum for aggravated trespass to $22,000 and three years.
- Farmers' concerns about trespass are real; so is the effect on what the public can see.
