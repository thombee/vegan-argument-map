Ask an adult to picture a farm and you will probably get the same scene: a barn, a paddock, a few animals wandering between them. Almost nobody saw this place in person. We learned it from songs, picture books, toys and cartoons, long before we could ask where our dinner came from.

This page is not about blaming anyone. Parents sing the songs because their parents did. The point is narrower: the farm we are taught as children and the farm that produces most of our food are different places, and the gap is one reason so few of us ever think about it.

## The farm in the book

The storybook farm has a handful of animals of every kind, each with a name and a personality. They live outdoors, they have a barn to sleep in, and they grow old. Nothing in the story gets sent anywhere. If there is a farmer, he is a kind of caretaker.

We did not choose to learn this version. It is simply the one our culture hands to small children, and it is a lovely one. The trouble is that it is often the only one we ever get.

## What the record shows

Our World in Data, drawing on Sentience Institute estimates, [puts the share of US livestock in factory farms at 99% in 2022, and the share of land animals worldwide at 74%](https://ourworldindata.org/how-many-animals-are-factory-farmed). It is careful to say that there is no official definition of a factory farm and that global data is thin, but concludes the evidence suggests most farm animals live this way. These are estimates, not a census.

In Australia, the RSPCA, an animal-welfare charity whose descriptions are worth reading alongside industry ones, says [most pigs are housed in intensive indoor systems](https://kb.rspca.org.au/knowledge-base/how-are-pigs-farmed-in-australia/) and are typically raised for five to six months before slaughter.

For chickens, [the same source](https://kb.rspca.org.au/knowledge-base/how-are-meat-chickens-farmed-in-australia) says meat chickens are typically slaughtered between four and six weeks of age, in sheds that can hold up to sixty thousand birds. A chicken's natural lifespan can be up to ten years. The song about the hen and her chicks has no verse for this.

Free-range and pasture systems exist, and some are better than the indoor sheds. That is worth saying plainly. But the RSPCA notes that meat chickens stay indoors until about three weeks old even where they later get outdoor access. For more on whether a kinder-sounding label changes the picture, see [humane meat](/a/humane-meat/) and [small farms](/a/small-farms/).

## Why the gap lasts

A children's book does not have to be false to leave something out. Stories are simplified, and they are rarely updated.

There is also the matter of what children are told at home. In one US study of 4 to 7 year olds, [many children placed foods like bacon in the plant category](https://www.sciencealert.com/a-surprising-number-of-kids-in-the-us-think-hotdogs-are-actually-vegetables), which we look at more closely in [Children don't start here](/why/children-dont-start-here/). If the book is the main thing a child has, the book becomes the picture.

None of this required a plan. It is what happens when most of us never see the places animals are raised, and the stories we pass on are not updated.

## In short

- The farm in songs and picture books is mostly outdoors, small and long-lived; most animals now live in intensive systems (estimates: 99% in the US, 74% of land animals globally).
- Australian meat chickens are typically slaughtered at four to six weeks and pigs at five to six months, according to the RSPCA.
- Better systems exist, but the book shows only the picture, not which system any given meal came from.
- Nobody was tricked by a nursery rhyme. The gap is inherited, which is also why it is easy to look at it fresh.
