Most people who eat meat also like animals. They would be upset to see a dog hurt, and many have a pet. Yet the same people eat pigs. Psychologists call this tension the meat paradox: [disliking harm to animals while enjoying eating them](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2010/07/100715091654.htm).

This page is about how people live with that tension. It is not an accusation. The research suggests that almost everyone does it, usually without noticing.

## Changing what we think the animal is

One way to resolve the paradox is to change your view of the animal. In 2012 Brock Bastian and colleagues ran three studies. [Animals seen as appropriate to eat were rated as having lower mental capacities. Meat eaters denied minds to food animals more when reminded of the link between meat and animal suffering, and expecting to eat meat soon increased this denial, which in turn reduced negative feelings](https://www.research.ed.ac.uk/en/publications/dont-mind-meat-the-denial-of-mind-to-animals-used-for-human-consu/).

An earlier study, according to [the press summary](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2010/07/100715091654.htm), found that when thoughts and actions conflict, people tend to adjust their moral standards rather than their behaviour, dampening "their moral consideration of animals when sitting at the dinner table." Notice what this is not: it is not lying. The shift happens quietly, and the person experiences it as simply how they see pigs.

## The four stock answers

When asked to justify eating meat, people tend to reach for the same replies. Matthew Piazza and colleagues named them the 4Ns: [natural, normal, necessary and nice](http://www.lancaster.ac.uk/news/articles/2015/how-people-defend-eating-meat/). In their studies the four categories covered most of the justifications people offered, and people who endorsed them attributed fewer mental capacities to cows. The authors argue the 4Ns work partly by reducing guilt.

Each N has a reply on this site. [It's natural](/a/its-natural/) and [ancestors ate meat](/a/ancestors-ate-meat/) take on the first, [culture and tradition](/a/culture-tradition/) the second, [protein](/a/protein/) and the health pages the third, and [taste](/a/taste-bacon/) the fourth. The point here is only that these answers are widely shared and seem to do a job for the person giving them.

## A name for the system

The social psychologist Melanie Joy gave this pattern a name, carnism. She defines it as [an invisible belief system that teaches us, from the moment we are weaned, not to feel disgusted](https://mercyforanimals.org/blog/the-mentality-of-meat-an-exclusive-interview-with-dr-melanie-joy/), and which makes some animals seem fit to eat while others seem unthinkable to eat. It is her term, and her framework is not a settled finding of the experimental research above. It is best read as a way of describing the same thing: that eating certain animals, and not others, is a convention we absorbed rather than a conclusion we reasoned our way to.

One piece of evidence that fits is that it is learned. Children in an English study were [about equally likely to classify pigs as pets or as food](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2022/04/220411101246.htm), while older participants were more likely to classify farm animals as food. The lead author put it as "dogs are our friends, pigs are food", judgements that "seem to largely depend on the species of the animal in question." We look at this in [the next page](/why/children-dont-start-here/).

## What follows

If the line between dog and pig is a convention, it can be examined like any other. The test is simple: what is the difference, relevant to being killed, between the two animals? [Where do you draw the line](/a/where-do-you-draw-the-line/) takes this on directly.

## In short

- The meat paradox is caring about animals while eating them; most people resolve it without noticing.
- Experiments find we rate animals we eat as having less mind, and more so when reminded of their suffering.
- The 4Ns (natural, normal, necessary, nice) cover most of the reasons people give for eating meat.
- "Carnism" is Melanie Joy's term for the unexamined system that decides which animals are food; it is a framework, not an experimental result.
