## The objection at full strength

Wool is not like meat. Shearing does not kill the sheep, and in fact a sheep that is not shorn can overheat and be badly affected by flystrike. Shearing is a service to the animal, the wool is a renewable fibre, and it replaces plastic-based synthetics.

## Response

I accept the point that, for the sheep we have, shearing is a service to the animal. Sheep carrying a full fleece into summer can suffer, and anyone who has taken in a rescued flock knows they need regular care.

The question is why they are like that. Sheep that shed their fleece do exist, but a CSIRO-published study found that [shedding Wiltshire Horn-Merino crosses produced far less wool than Merinos](https://doi.org/10.1071/EA9940717), which helps explain why wool flocks are not bred to shed. The same study found [blowfly strike far more common in Merinos](https://doi.org/10.1071/EA9940717) than in the half-Merino crosses. Much of the need for shearing and for fly protection, in other words, follows from breeding for wool yield.

That trade-off has a visible cost. Mulesing, the practice of cutting skin from a lamb's breech to reduce flystrike, is carried out on [millions of Australian lambs each year, typically at 6 to 10 weeks old and without prior anaesthesia](https://kb.rspca.org.au/knowledge-base/what-is-the-rspcas-view-on-mulesing-and-flystrike-prevention-in-sheep/). The RSPCA says it is unacceptable to keep breeding sheep that need it.

Existing sheep should be shorn and cared for. The vegan position is not to keep breeding more of them for fibre.

## In short

- Modern wool sheep do need shearing, because they were bred that way.
- Mulesing is performed on millions of Australian lambs a year, usually without prior anaesthesia.
- The RSPCA says it is unacceptable to keep breeding sheep that need mulesing.
- The kind thing for existing sheep is shearing; the consistent thing is not to demand more.
