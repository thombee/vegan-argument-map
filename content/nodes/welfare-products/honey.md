## The objection at full strength

Bees make honey in surplus, the beekeeper takes some, and the colony carries on. Nobody is killed for it, and a hive is arguably a mutual arrangement: the bees get shelter and protection, the keeper gets some honey. Bees are insects, with tiny brains. If any animal product is harmless, it is this one.

## Response

This is the animal product with the least obvious harm, and a reasonable person could think it is trivial. There is no abattoir and no calf. The reason to avoid it rests on a smaller set of points.

**We do not know that bees do not feel.** The 2024 New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness, signed by a number of researchers, states that the evidence indicates [a realistic possibility of conscious experience in many invertebrates, including insects](https://sites.google.com/nyu.edu/nydeclaration/declaration). A realistic possibility is not proof, but it is a reason for caution, especially when the benefit to us is a spread for toast. The wider question is covered at [What about insects, oysters and mussels?](/a/insects-and-bivalves/).

**Honey is the bees' food.** Honey is what the colony stores to get through lean periods. Beekeepers commonly replace the harvested honey with sugar: a 2023 paper in the journal *Animals* notes that [harvested honey is usually replaced by an alternative sugar to overwinter colonies](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani13030323). Colonies on sugar fare adequately in the studies, so this is not an animal-welfare disaster, but it does mean the bees' own food is being substituted for our benefit.

Honey is also one of the easiest product to replace, with maple syrup, agave and other syrups. This is a case where the principle is "if I don't need to take it, I won't", and it costs little.

## In short

- Honey is a minor harm, but it is the bees' own food source.
- Insects may be able to feel; the evidence suggests a realistic possibility.
- Harvested honey is typically replaced with sugar.
- It is an easy product to substitute.
