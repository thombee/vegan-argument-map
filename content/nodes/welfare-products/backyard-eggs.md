## The objection at full strength

My hens live in the garden. They scratch, dust-bathe, roost, and have names. I collect the eggs they would lay anyway, and no chick is ever harmed because the eggs are not fertilised. This is nothing like a battery farm, and it feels like the one animal product that costs nothing.

## Response

This is the most defensible animal product there is, and there is no comparison between your hens and a caged flock. If your hens are rescued, or live out their lives with you, that is a good thing.

Two things are still worth knowing.

**The hens themselves were bred for laying.** Commercial laying breeds have been selected for high egg output. In a small pilot study, [69% of White Leghorn hens (a laying breed) had keel bone fractures at 80 weeks, against 10% of Red Jungle fowl hens](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0255234) kept in the same facility. The sample was small and the study was a pilot, so it does not prove the cause, but it fits a wider concern about the cost of heavy laying to the hen's body. Eggs are not free to the hen.

**The chicks came from somewhere.** If your hens were bought as chicks from a commercial hatchery that supplies the egg industry, then in that industry [the vast majority of male chicks are killed on the day they hatch](https://kb.rspca.org.au/knowledge-base/what-happens-with-male-chicks-in-the-egg-industry/). Buying young hens from that supply supports it, even if the hens themselves are well treated.

None of this is an accusation. A backyard keeper who adopts ex-industry hens has done more for chickens than most people. The most consistent version is to keep the hens as companions and not rely on the eggs, or not to buy new birds.

## In short

- Backyard hens are far better off than caged hens, and rescuing them is a good thing.
- Laying breeds pay a physical cost for high egg output.
- Hatcheries that supply hens also kill most male chicks.
- Adopting ex-industry hens avoids adding to demand.
