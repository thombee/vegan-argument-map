## The objection at full strength

A good shearer takes a few minutes per sheep, and the animal walks away lighter and cooler. There is no killing, no blood, and the sheep goes back to the paddock. If this is cruelty, then so is cutting your own hair.

## Response

Shearing is probably the least troubling part of the wool industry, and it is fair to say so. A skilled shearer working with calm sheep does a good job.

It is still not a haircut. RSPCA Australia describes shearing as [an acute stressor, with sheep handled several times on the day through mustering, yarding and penning](https://kb.rspca.org.au/knowledge-base/what-are-the-animal-welfare-issues-with-shearing-of-sheep/). It adds that nicks and cuts can happen when a sheep struggles or the shearer is rushed, and that pain is a concern when sheep are wounded. It also notes that when sheep are positioned well they do not tend to resist, which is what the best shearers aim for. A person chooses their haircut; a sheep does not.

That is a moderate concern. It does not make shearing a reason by itself to avoid wool. The bigger issues are elsewhere: the practices such as [mulesing](/a/wool/) that the industry has used to manage the sheep it has bred, and the choice to keep breeding them. A rescued sheep that needs a trim is a different matter from a commercial flock.

## In short

- Shearing is the mildest part of the industry, but it is not stress-free.
- RSPCA Australia lists handling stress and the risk of cuts as the main shearing concerns.
- The larger welfare costs are elsewhere, as set out in the main answer.
