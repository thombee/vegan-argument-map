## The objection at full strength

Mulesing is an old problem and the industry knows it. Many growers have stopped, pain relief products are available, and I buy wool with non-mulesed certification. Treating the whole industry as unchanged is out of date.

## Response

Non-mulesed wool is better than mulesed wool, and buying it is a reasonable choice. Some producers have already moved away from it, and the RSPCA itself urges retailers to buy only non-mulesed wool.

The RSPCA's current view is that progress is slower than it should be. It says [millions of lambs are mulesed each year in Australia](https://kb.rspca.org.au/knowledge-base/what-is-the-rspcas-view-on-mulesing-and-flystrike-prevention-in-sheep/), that pain relief is not always used, and that because the industry has not prioritised breeding for flystrike resistance, a large proportion of Merinos are still mulesed. It also says that changing a flock through breeding can take from a few years to more than ten.

That is a reason to be cautious about the phrase "being phased out". It describes a direction, not a finished change. And a certification only guarantees what it measures. It says nothing about the other costs of wool, such as the handling and the end of the animal's life.

You can support the right direction and still reach the broader conclusion: a change that takes a decade or more to work through a flock is slow, and that is a fair reason to doubt that welfare will always get priority.

## In short

- Non-mulesed wool is better than mulesed wool.
- The RSPCA says a large proportion of Merinos are still mulesed and that breeding solutions have not been prioritised.
- A certification covers one practice, not the whole animal's life.
