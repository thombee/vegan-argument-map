## The objection at full strength

If the problem is overfishing, aquaculture is the answer. The FAO reports that farmed production has overtaken wild catch, and each farmed fish is one that the sea did not have to supply. Farming fish is how we can eat seafood without emptying the oceans.

## Response

Aquaculture has indeed become the main source of aquatic animals. FAO reports that in 2022 it [surpassed capture fisheries for the first time, supplying 51% of total aquatic animal production](https://www.fao.org/newsroom/detail/fao-report-global-fisheries-and-aquaculture-production-reaches-a-new-record-high/en).

Wild catch is not only eaten directly. A 2024 analysis notes that wild fish are caught for food, bait and feed, often as fishmeal and oil, and estimated that the number used for reduction to fishmeal and oil [represented 56% of the total 2010 estimate of wild finfish caught](https://doi.org/10.1017/awf.2024.7) (by midpoint). That covers all uses of fishmeal and oil, not aquaculture alone, so it does not tell us how much of it feeds farmed fish. It does show that a large share of the wild catch is not caught for direct human consumption.

And the objection has changed the subject from sustainability to supply. Farmed fish are still animals with an interest in not being killed, raised in conditions that vary. The ethical question is the same, and it is in addition to the environmental one.

If your priority is the ocean, and if your priority is the fish, the same answer applies: eat neither wild nor farmed fish.

## In short

- Aquaculture now supplies more than half of aquatic animals.
- A large share of wild-caught fish goes to fishmeal and oil rather than direct human consumption.
- Farmed fish are still killed, so the welfare question remains.
