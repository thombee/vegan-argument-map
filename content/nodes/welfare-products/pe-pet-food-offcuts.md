## The objection at full strength

Pet food uses the parts of animals people do not eat. If a cat eats those, no extra animal was killed for the cat. Feeding a pet is more like recycling than demand for meat.

## Response

Some pet food does draw on by-products, and that reduces waste. This is a reasonable point and I cannot give you a precise share for Australia.

But as a general picture, the pet food system is not small. A 2017 analysis of the US found that dogs and cats consumed [about 33% as much animal-derived energy as humans do](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0181301), and accounted for roughly 25 to 30% of the environmental impacts of animal production in terms of land, water, fossil fuel, phosphate and biocides. That is a US figure from one study. It suggests pet food is a significant part of animal agriculture's demand, not just its offcuts.

The related point is that the pet food market gives value to those parts, which supports the industries that produce them. This is the same dynamic discussed in [Leather is just a by-product](/a/leather-byproduct/).

Where a cat truly needs animal-derived food, that remains a constraint to manage. It is a reason to adopt carefully and to choose well, not a reason to treat it as no impact at all.

## In short

- Some pet food uses by-products, but we do not have a firm figure for Australia.
- A US analysis found dogs and cats consume about a third as much animal-derived energy as people.
- A cat's real dietary needs are a constraint to manage, not a reason to ignore the footprint.
