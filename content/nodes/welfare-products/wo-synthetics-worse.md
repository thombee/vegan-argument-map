## The objection at full strength

If you reject wool you end up in polyester and acrylic. Those are plastics made from oil, and they pollute waterways with microfibres every time they are washed. A natural, biodegradable fibre like wool is better for the environment.

## Response

The microfibre concern is real. Napper and Thompson's laboratory study found that polyester, polyester-cotton and acrylic fabrics all released fibres when washed, and estimated that [more than 700,000 fibres could be released from an average 6 kg load of acrylic](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.marpolbul.2016.09.025). A vegan who buys synthetic knitwear is not exempt from that concern.

But synthetics are not the only alternative. Cotton, linen, hemp and other plant fibres exist, and the same study found that the polyester-cotton blend [shed significantly fewer fibres](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.marpolbul.2016.09.025) than polyester or acrylic. Choosing second-hand and well-made clothing reduces the problem under any fibre.

Environmental comparisons between wool and its alternatives are contested and depend on what is measured. They do not settle the question of whether to use animals when other options exist. A fabric can be better in one respect and still involve an animal that was bred for it.

## In short

- Synthetic fibres do shed microfibres in the wash.
- Plant-based and second-hand options exist.
- An environmental comparison does not remove the question of the animal's interests.
