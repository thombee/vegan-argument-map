## The objection at full strength

Not everyone is going to go vegan tomorrow. If I and millions like me switch to free-range and higher-welfare products, producers have to respond. Welfare reforms like cage-free eggs have only happened because consumers asked for them. Demanding perfection risks making the better the enemy of the good.

## Response

Consumer pressure has improved some conditions, and that matters to the animals affected. It is a fair point that welfare campaigns, including from organisations such as the RSPCA, have pushed industry on specific practices.

Two cautions apply. First, the sorts of standards that sit behind labels can be thin. The legal free-range egg limit in Australia is [10,000 hens per hectare](https://www.foodauthority.nsw.gov.au/food-labelling/understanding-claims/free-range), so a purchase that feels like a strong stand may be backing a modest minimum. Second, a purchase is also a signal that the product is acceptable. The RSPCA has urged the egg industry to stop killing day-old male chicks, yet the practice continues, and [it describes alternatives as available](https://kb.rspca.org.au/knowledge-base/what-happens-with-male-chicks-in-the-egg-industry/). A comfortable middle choice can reduce the pressure to finish the job.

None of this means you should wait for perfection. Higher-welfare purchasing and veganism are not rivals in the way the objection suggests. The honest position is that welfare reforms are worth having, and that the logic that drives them (that the animals' experience matters) points further than the reforms go.

## In short

- Welfare purchasing and reform campaigns have made some real differences.
- Labels set minimums that are often lower than people imagine.
- Reform that leaves the killing in place cannot answer the question of whether the killing is justified.
