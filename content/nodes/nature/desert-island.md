## The objection at full strength

You're stranded with nothing but fish and a spear. Would you starve rather than eat? If you'd eat, the objection goes, then your principle isn't absolute, and the rest is just a matter of where you draw the line.

## Response

Most vegans would eat to survive, and most people would say they were right to. That doesn't damage the case, because it never said the principle is exceptionless. The Vegan Society's definition describes avoiding animal exploitation [as far as is possible and practicable](https://vegansociety.com/go-vegan/definition-veganism). Real necessity falls outside that.

Moral rules that bend under extreme necessity are common. Most of us think it is wrong to steal, but a starving person taking bread is a different case. Nobody concludes theft is fine in general.

The test the island case gives us is useful: it separates killing out of need from killing out of preference. Almost no one reading this faces a choice between death and eating animals. When we can live well without the harm, the island is not the situation we are in.

## In short

- Emergency necessity can justify what ordinary life can't.
- Veganism is about what is possible and practicable, not martyrdom.
- The island doesn't resemble a supermarket.
