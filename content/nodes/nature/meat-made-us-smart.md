## The objection at full strength

Anthropologists have long argued that eating meat, a calorie- and nutrient-dense food, helped our ancestors grow bigger brains while their guts shrank. Without animal food, the argument goes, we would not be the species capable of philosophy, or of veganism.

## Response

This is a real debate, and parts of it may well be true. A 2016 paper in Frontiers in Neuroscience that argued against the cooking explanation still [favoured dietary shifts toward seeds and meat, together with tool use](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4842772) as drivers of higher energy intake. So eating animals likely played some part in our history, and we should say so honestly.

But the story is not settled. The "expensive tissue" idea says brain growth was paid for by a smaller gut, and the same paper notes that [later work found no such trade-off across 100 mammal species](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4842772). Scientists still argue about the role of cooking, plants, tubers, tools and social learning.

More important, even a complete account of the past would not tell us what to do now. A reason that was good for our ancestors, when food was scarce, is not a reason for people with supermarkets. And if intelligence matters morally, the fact that it was built partly from animals' lives is not a reason to keep taking them.

## In short

- Meat probably played some role in our evolution; the details are still debated.
- How we got big brains doesn't tell us we need meat today.
- The history can't justify killing animals we don't need to kill.
