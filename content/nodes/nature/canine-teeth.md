## The objection at full strength

Look in the mirror. We have incisors, canines and molars, a mixed set of teeth like other omnivores and unlike pure herbivores such as cows. Our canines are a sign that our ancestors tore at flesh.

## Response

Human canines are small compared with those of true carnivores, and they are not evidence of a meat diet. A comparison of primates and carnivores found that [primate male canines are on average as large as or larger than those of similarly sized carnivores, and seed-eating primates have relatively strong canines](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18186502/) (Plavcan & Ruff, 2008). Many plant-eating primates have formidable canines; so a canine tooth can't be read as a sign that its owner eats meat.

We can agree on a smaller point: humans are omnivores. Our teeth and gut can handle both plants and animal foods. But being able to eat something is a statement about what we can do, not what we must do or what we should do. An omnivore can thrive on plants alone, as the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics says of [well-planned vegan diets](https://digitalcommons.andrews.edu/pubs/648).

Even a perfect set of meat-tearing teeth would not settle the ethics. Our moral question is about whether killing is justified when we do not need to do it.

## In short

- Canines are common in plant-eating primates, so they don't prove a meat diet.
- We're omnivores; that means we can eat meat, not that we must or should.
