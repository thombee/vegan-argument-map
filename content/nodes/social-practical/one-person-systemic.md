## The objection at full strength

Even if individual choice matters a little, the big levers are subsidies, regulation and industry practice. Telling individuals to change their plates is a way of avoiding the harder political work. If you want results, push for laws.

## Response

Policy does matter, and a lot of the large-scale levers are collective. Few people would argue against campaigning for better rules.

But the two do not compete. Laws change when enough people want them to, and enough people want them when they have thought about the issue and often when they have already changed what they do. Markets for new products appear when people buy them. Waiting for governments to move first usually means waiting for the people who elect those governments to move first.

It is also not an either-or choice for most individuals. You can eat differently, talk about it and vote or advocate for reform. The objection offers a real strategy, "do the policy work", and then treats it as a reason to skip the part that is already in your hands.

## In short

- Policy and personal change feed each other.
- Governments move when voters and markets do.
- You can do both.
