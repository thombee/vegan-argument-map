## The objection at full strength

Have you seen the price of oat milk, vegan cheese and plant-based mince? Not everyone can afford specialty products or shop at the right supermarket. Telling people to go vegan is telling them to spend more when they can barely cover groceries. It's a diet for people with money.

## Response

You are right about one thing: branded vegan substitutes, meat-free burgers and speciality cheeses are often dearer than the basics. If a diet means buying those every day, it can cost more.

But those products aren't what a vegan diet has to be built on. The [Oxford study published in The Lancet Planetary Health in 2021](https://www.leap.ox.ac.uk/article/sustainable-eating-is-cheaper-as-well-as-healthier-reveals-new-study) modelled food costs for healthy diets around the world. In high-income countries, vegan diets were the cheapest option, cutting food costs by up to a third (21 to 34%, depending on the foods chosen). The study looked at whole foods and excluded highly processed meat replacements, restaurant meals and takeaways, which is exactly where the price differences in the objection come from.

The same study is clear about the limits. In lower-income countries, healthy sustainable diets were more expensive than people's current diets, so the picture is not the same everywhere. It is a model of prices, not your local shop, so availability and your own budget count.

Whole-food staples such as beans, lentils, rice, oats and vegetables are what the study's cheaper diets were built from. For many people, the answer to "is it too expensive?" depends on whether they buy the staples or the substitutes.

## In short

- Processed vegan substitutes can be pricey.
- In high-income countries, whole-food vegan diets were the cheapest in the Oxford study.
- In lower-income countries the picture is different.
