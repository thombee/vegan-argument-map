## The objection at full strength

I've got nothing against what people eat. But some vegans turn every meal into a lecture, post graphic videos, and treat everyone around them as guilty. Even people who agree with the cause come away irritated. If you want to change minds, shouting at people makes them dig in. That reputation is earned.

## Response

Some of it is earned, and it is fair to say so. Moralising at someone who didn't ask, shaming a person over a menu choice, or treating friends as opponents is poor manners and also poor strategy. If you have been on the receiving end, your irritation is reasonable.

Two things are still worth separating, though. First, the manner of a messenger is a different matter from the truth of the message. A rude person can be right and a charming one wrong, so "annoying" cannot be the reason to dismiss the argument itself.

Second, there is research on why the reaction is so strong. In studies by [Minson and Monin](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2011/12/Minson---Monin,-2011.pdf), 47% of meat-eaters freely associated negative terms with vegetarians, and the negativity was greater among those who expected vegetarians to feel morally superior. The authors argue this is partly resentment at *anticipated* moral judgment, even when none has been expressed. That means some of the "preachiness" is perceived rather than present, and it is not all one side's fault.

The practical answer is for both sides to behave decently: vegans should be kind, patient and ask before sharing opinions, and everyone else can notice whether the person in front of them is actually lecturing or is simply not eating the dish. The question of whether animals are ours to use remains whichever way that goes.

## In short

- Some advocacy is off-putting; that is a fair criticism.
- Tone doesn't decide whether an argument is right.
- Research suggests anticipated judgment drives part of the reaction.
