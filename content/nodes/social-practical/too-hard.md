## The objection at full strength

I've tried to cut back on animal products. Labels hide milk and egg in everything, restaurants have one sad option, family meals become arguments, and I'm cooking something different from everyone else. Add the need to plan your nutrition, and I just don't have the time or energy for it. It's not that I don't care; it's that daily life is full enough.

## Response

That is a fair description of the first few months, and it is worth saying plainly that the effort is real. Learning new recipes, reading labels and handling awkward social moments is work. Anyone who tells you it is effortless is overselling it.

Three things make the picture less bleak. First, the difficulty is front-loaded. Once you have a dozen meals you like, shopping becomes routine, as it did for whatever you cook now. Second, the nutrition side is manageable. The [Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics](https://digitalcommons.andrews.edu/pubs/648) says appropriately planned vegan diets are healthful and nutritionally adequate at all stages of life, and the [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/eat-well/how-to-eat-a-balanced-diet/the-vegetarian-diet/) notes the main must-do: vitamin B12 is found naturally only in animal products, so you need a reliable source such as fortified foods.

Third, the comparison is lopsided. The inconvenience falls on the person for a while; the cost of not changing falls on animals for their whole lives. Inconvenience is a real reason to go gradually, and it is a reason for patience with yourself. It is not much of a reason to conclude that the practice is acceptable. The Vegan Society's own definition includes "as far as is possible and practicable", so nobody is asking you to be perfect on day one.

## In short

- The effort is real, especially early on.
- The main nutrition step is a reliable source of B12.
- Inconvenience argues for going step by step, not for stopping.
