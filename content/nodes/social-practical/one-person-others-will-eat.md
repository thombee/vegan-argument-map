## The objection at full strength

Say I stop buying beef. The price drops a bit, and someone else buys it. Nothing is saved; it just goes to a different customer. The animal's already dead before I ever decide.

## Response

This is the best form of the objection, and it is partly right: if a drop in demand lowers prices, other buyers may buy a little more. Economists describe this as a dampening of the effect.

The key word is "partly". For the displacement to be total, others would have to absorb exactly the amount you stopped buying, at no cost. In practice, lower demand means lower prices only if producers keep producing, and producers who earn less produce less. The effect is spread out and smaller than one-for-one, but it is not zero, and it grows as more people act in the same direction.

On the point about the animal already being dead: shops order based on what they expect to sell. Sales data guides what is bred, raised and shipped next. Your choices are not about this particular animal; they are about the next order.

The cleanest way to see it is that you are deciding whether you participate. That stands whether or not the market fully corrects for it.

## In short

- Displacement is partial, not total.
- Producers respond to sales over time.
- Your choice also settles whether you take part.
