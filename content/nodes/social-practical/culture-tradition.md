## The objection at full strength

Food is how my family and community show love. The Sunday roast, the holiday ham, the dishes my grandmother made are tied up with who I am. Asking me to drop them is asking me to drop part of my heritage, and I think cultures should be treated with respect, not lectured by outsiders.

## Response

Food and belonging really are bound together, and the sense of loss behind this objection is genuine. A meal can hold generations of memory, and nobody should wave that away.

The difficulty is that "this is our tradition" describes a practice but doesn't justify it. Cultures have kept many practices that were later recognised as wrong, with people raised inside them defending them in exactly these terms. The [Slavery Abolition Act 1833](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Will4/3-4/73/contents) ended a long-standing lawful practice across the British dominions; heritage can explain why a practice feels important. It cannot also answer the question of whether the practice causes harm.

It is also worth noticing how often traditions move. Cultures also have long plant-based strands: [Pew's survey in India](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2021/06/29/religion-in-india-tolerance-and-segregation/) found 44% of Hindus and 92% of Jains describe themselves as vegetarian.

In practice, people often keep the ritual and change the ingredient: the gathering, the recipe's spices, the table, the story. What carries a tradition is usually the people and the meaning, not any single animal product.

## In short

- Heritage explains why a practice matters; it doesn't show it's right.
- Past traditions, such as slavery, were defended the same way.
- Plant-based traditions are long-standing in several cultures.
