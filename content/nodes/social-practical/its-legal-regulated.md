## The objection at full strength

I accept legal isn't everything. But we do have animal welfare laws: cruelty is an offence, slaughter is regulated, and there are standards for housing. The system already puts limits on the worst. Isn't that precisely what a moral society does?

## Response

Welfare laws are a real improvement and are worth defending. A society that sets standards is better than one that does not, and campaigners have achieved genuine gains.

But look at what such laws do. They regulate how animals are treated while allowing the practice they are part of. They do not question whether it is acceptable to raise an animal in order to kill it for a meal that most of us do not need. Laws that regulate how something is done are not the same as laws that say it is right.

Recent legislation shows the direction of thinking. The UK's [Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act 2022](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2022/22/contents) defines "animal" to include vertebrates, cephalopod molluscs and decapod crustaceans. If the law recognises that these animals are sentient, the pressing question is whether killing them for taste can be defended in the first place, which is the question on this site.

You can hold both views: that welfare laws are better than none, and that they do not make the underlying practice fine. For the specific claims about "humane" products, see [I only buy humane / free-range / local](/a/humane-meat/).

## In short

- Welfare laws limit treatment; they do not justify killing.
- Regulation is not the same as moral approval.
- Recognising sentience raises the question of killing.
