## The objection at full strength

Genesis says humans are made in God's image and given dominion over the fish, the birds and every living creature. After the flood, God says that every moving thing that lives shall be food for us. Christians and Jews have eaten meat for millennia. If you take scripture seriously, meat is permitted, and asking believers to give it up is asking them to set aside what God said.

## Response

This is an honest objection and it deserves a serious answer, not a dismissal. Many believers hold the view sincerely, and the text does say what is quoted. [Genesis 1:26](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+1%3A26-31%3B+Genesis+2%3A15&version=NRSV) grants dominion, and [Genesis 9:3](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+9%3A1-4&version=NRSV) says "every moving thing that lives shall be food for you."

What the reading leaves out is the rest of the same story. In Genesis 1:29-30 the first diet given to humans and animals is plants. In 2:15 the man is placed in the garden "to till it and keep it", and many readers take dominion in light of that. Genesis 9:4 permits meat after the flood but forbids eating flesh with its blood, a limit on the permission, not an endorsement.

Within the Catholic tradition, Pope Francis writes in [Laudato Si'](https://www.vatican.va/content/francesco/en/encyclicals/documents/papa-francesco_20150524_enciclica-laudato-si.html) that dominion does not justify "absolute domination over other creatures," that "keeping" means care, and that other creatures have "a value of their own in God's eyes."

So the question for the believer is not whether meat is allowed, but whether permission is the same as obligation, or as a good reason in a world where most of us can eat well without it. Pope Francis, for one, reads stewardship as pointing toward gentleness. A permission, in other words, can be declined as an act of care.

## In short

- Dominion in context is paired with 'till and keep'.
- The first diet in Genesis is plants.
- A permission isn't a command; many traditions read dominion as care.
