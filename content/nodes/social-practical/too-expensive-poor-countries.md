## The objection at full strength

Your savings are for rich countries. Many households live somewhere without a decent supermarket, with limited fresh food, or with so little money that any change is risky. For some families animal foods are the most affordable source of calories and protein. A middle-class argument doesn't apply to them.

## Response

The study behind the cost claim says the same. In lower-income countries such as those in sub-Saharan Africa and the Indian subcontinent, the Oxford researchers found healthy, sustainable diets were at least a third more expensive than current diets. So this is not a point to brush aside, and food insecurity is a serious problem.

Two caveats are worth adding. First, a model of diets is not a description of what each household eats or can reach. Second, the moral argument is addressed to people who have a real choice. Veganism is defined as excluding exploitation of animals "[as far as is possible and practicable](https://www.vegansociety.com/go-vegan/definition-veganism)", and practicability includes whether you can afford and reach the food. Someone with no alternative is not the target.

That leaves most readers in wealthy countries, where the study found a vegan diet cheapest, and where this objection does not apply to them.

## In short

- The Oxford study found costs higher in lower-income countries.
- Veganism is defined 'as far as is possible and practicable'.
- The argument targets people with a real choice.
