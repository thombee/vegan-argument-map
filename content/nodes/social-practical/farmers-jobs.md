## The objection at full strength

Farming is not just an industry; it is families, small towns and a way of life that has been handed down. If everyone stopped buying animal products, rural communities would be hollowed out. People who do hard, honest work would lose their incomes and their land. You can't care about animals and ignore these people.

## Response

This is a serious concern and it should be treated as one. Farmers are not villains. Many work long hours for modest returns in a system that squeezes them as much as it squeezes the animals, and a change in demand would have real costs for real communities. Any honest account of a shift away from animal farming has to include support for the people affected.

What the argument cannot do, however, is settle the moral question. Almost every practice we have ended employed people: if the jobs involved had been enough to justify them, none of those practices could ever have been abolished. The question of whether a practice is wrong has to come first, and the question of how to treat those who earn a living from it comes next.

There is evidence that farmers can and do change. A [Frontiers in Sustainable Food Systems study](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fsufs.2023.1122992/full) collected 27 accounts of farms leaving livestock. Some moved from intensive poultry or pig farming to mushroom or market gardening for economic reasons, and others did so out of compassion, mostly to animal sanctuaries or market gardening. The sample is small, so it shows that a path exists, not how easy it is.

Demand also changes gradually, not overnight, which gives time for support schemes, retraining and new markets.

## In short

- Farmers deserve support; a transition should include them.
- Jobs don't make a practice right: that would have blocked every reform.
- Some farms have already moved to other work.
