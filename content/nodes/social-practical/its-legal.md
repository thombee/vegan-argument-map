## The objection at full strength

Eating meat is legal, regulated and supported by the state. We have inspections, welfare codes and enforcement. If the practice were truly wrong, a democratic society would have banned it. Citizens who obey the law and follow the rules aren't doing anything unethical.

## Response

Obeying the law is a good default, and regulated farming is better than unregulated farming. But the step from "legal" to "ethical" does not hold, and almost everyone knows it when the example is different.

Slavery is the standard case. Under British law it was lawful across the empire until the [Slavery Abolition Act 1833](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Will4/3-4/73/contents) provided for its abolition. People who owned or traded in slaves before then broke no law, and yet we do not conclude it was acceptable. Laws are made by people, often with the interests of the powerful in mind, and they lag behind moral understanding. 

Law also tends to follow ethical argument, not precede it. Every reform had to start from someone saying "this is legal, and it is still wrong." A related point is that the law about animals is itself shifting: the UK's [Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act 2022](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2022/22/contents) created an Animal Sentience Committee, which reflects growing recognition of what animals are.

None of that makes law irrelevant. It sets the rules we live by and deserves respect. It just can't be where the moral question ends.

## In short

- Legal is not the same as right; slavery was lawful until 1833.
- Laws lag behind moral argument.
- Reform starts from 'legal, but wrong'.
