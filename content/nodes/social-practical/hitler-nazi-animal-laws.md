## The objection at full strength

The Nazis banned foie gras, restricted animal experiments and required humane slaughter. That is a regime that put animals ahead of people. Isn't that where animal rights thinking can end up?

## Response

The laws existed. According to [Herzog](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/animals-and-us/201111/was-hitler-a-vegetarian-the-nazi-animal-protection-movement), soon after taking power in 1933 the Nazis enacted many animal protection laws, some still in effect in Germany, including penalties for mistreating pets, a foie gras ban, limits on invasive animal research and humane slaughter rules. He also notes that a 1942 law banning Jews from keeping pets is among the most chilling episodes, since Jewish people were not covered by the protections applied to animals.

That is the lesson: concern for animals proved no safeguard against cruelty to people, because that regime applied concern selectively. Herzog describes the contrast as a moral inversion. The conclusion is that care must be consistent, not that care for animals is suspect.

The argument for animals is that suffering matters wherever it occurs, which is the opposite of the selective approach the Nazis followed.

## In short

- The laws existed, applied selectively.
- Selective compassion is the failure, not compassion itself.
- Consistent concern for suffering is what the vegan argument asks for.
