## The objection at full strength

I've halved my meat, switched to oat milk, and I have a few meat-free days a week. For most people that's a big change. Going fully vegan seems like all-or-nothing thinking, and it puts people off doing anything. Isn't it better to have millions of people reducing than a few perfect vegans?

## Response

Start with what is right about this. Cutting down is genuinely good. It spares animals, lowers environmental impact, and it is how most people actually change. A million people eating less meat do more than a handful of strict vegans, and anyone who sneers at your reductions is being unhelpful. If you have halved your intake, that is a real achievement.

The case for going the rest of the way is a moral one. If animals are not ours to use, then the question for each meal is the same as for the last one: is there a good reason to do this to a being who can suffer? Cutting down shrinks how often you answer yes, but the answer, when you do eat them, is still the same one. We rarely accept "I've cut down" as an end point for other wrongs. Nobody thinks "I only hit my dog on weekends" is the stopping place.

There is also the environmental side. In [modelling by Springmann and colleagues](https://www.oxfordmartin.ox.ac.uk/news/201603-plant-based-diets), vegan diets cut food-related greenhouse gas emissions by 70% by 2050 and avoided 8.1 million deaths a year, so the further steps matter.

This isn't an invitation to guilt. It is an invitation to treat "less" as a good place to be on the way, and to keep going while you are still moving.

## In short

- Cutting down is good and worth celebrating.
- If animals aren't ours to use, 'less' is a step, not the end.
- The further steps still matter for animals and climate.
