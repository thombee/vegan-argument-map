## The objection at full strength

Vegans still drive on roads where animals die, use phones with mined materials, eat crops harvested with machinery, and take medicines tested on animals. If you can't avoid all harm, the line you draw is arbitrary. You just prefer your own comfortable compromises to mine, so spare me the lecture about purity.

## Response

This is a fair thing to notice, and many vegans agree that perfect harmlessness is impossible. That is why the [Vegan Society's definition](https://www.vegansociety.com/go-vegan/definition-veganism) says veganism seeks to exclude exploitation of and cruelty to animals "as far as is possible and practicable." The word "practicable" was put there deliberately: veganism is a direction, not a claim of purity.

The core logic is a familiar one. If you can't avoid all harm, it doesn't follow that you should avoid none. Someone who can't eliminate pollution still sensibly cuts what they can. A doctor who can't save every patient still treats the ones in front of her. Failing to be perfect is a poor reason to give up on being better.

Also, the harms listed are of a different kind. Eating a steak is a deliberate, avoidable purchase of an animal product. A death on the road is an unintended side effect of driving. Intention and avoidability matter in nearly all of ethics, which is why we treat murder differently from a fatal accident.

Finally, even if vegans were all hypocrites, the argument would be unaffected. A consistent argument can be delivered by an inconsistent person. If you spot a gap in a vegan's practice, that gives you reason to ask them about it, not a reason to think the animals matter less.

## In short

- Veganism is defined 'as far as is possible and practicable'.
- Not avoiding all harm doesn't mean avoiding none.
- A messenger's flaws don't change the argument.
