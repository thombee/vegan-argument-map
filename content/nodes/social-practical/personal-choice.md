## The objection at full strength

What I eat is about as personal as a decision gets. It touches my body, my family, my culture and my budget. Nobody elected vegans to run my kitchen, and I don't tell them how to live. A free society works because people can hold different values and leave each other alone. If I'm not hurting you, why can't I just get on with my dinner?

## Response

Most of us agree with the principle behind this. Where a choice affects only the chooser, it really is theirs: what you wear, how you spend a weekend, which music you like. That is why this objection feels so natural.

The difficulty is that a meal of animal products is not self-contained. Someone else is at the other end of the choice: an animal who was bred, kept and killed so the meal could exist. When a choice has a victim, we do not usually call it purely personal. Almost nobody says "dog fighting is a personal choice" or "how I treat my dog is nobody's business." We accept that the question changes the moment another being's interests are in play. The real argument is therefore about whether farmed animals count, which is covered in the moral status pages, not about whether choices are private.

Nor is explaining a view the same as forcing it. Saying "I think this is wrong, and here is why" is how every moral change has spread, from abolition to child labour laws. You are free to listen, disagree and walk away. What the objection cannot do is turn a disagreement about whether animals matter into a rule that the topic is off limits.

That said, the way a view is shared matters. Nobody is obliged to hear it at every dinner, and good advocacy respects that: see [vegans are preachy and annoying](/a/preachy-vegans/).

## In short

- A choice that ends another being's life is not only personal.
- We don't call dog fighting a personal choice either.
- Making an argument is persuasion, not coercion.
