## The objection at full strength

Morality is what rational agents agree to for mutual benefit. Animals cannot agree to anything and cannot keep their side of a bargain, so they sit outside the agreement. We might choose to be kind to them, but we are not bound to be.

## Response

The contract view is one of the most influential in moral philosophy, and it deserves a serious reply, not a dismissal.

The first problem is that it threatens to exclude too much. Newborns and people with severe cognitive disabilities cannot enter bargains either. The [Stanford Encyclopedia](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/moral-animal/) lists the proposal to treat non-persons as indirectly considerable among the standard responses to this marginal-cases problem, which amounts to adding a second layer of duties.

The second problem is that the additional duties tend to cover animals too. Once you accept that something can be owed to a being that cannot reciprocate, the question is what grounds the duty, and the plausible answer is that the being can be harmed.

The result is that the contract tells us what we owe each other, and leaves open what else is owed. It is not an argument that nothing is.

## In short

- Contract theory has to explain infants and disabled adults.
- Whatever it adds to cover them tends to reach animals too.
- It tells us what we owe each other, not what is owed to no one else.
