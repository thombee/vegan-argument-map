## The objection at full strength

Consciousness is private. We know we suffer because we feel it, and we infer it in other humans because they are like us and can tell us. A chicken or a pig cannot report anything, and its brain is very different from ours. Careful people, not just cynics, say we should be cautious about reading human feelings into animals, and that behaviour that looks like pain can be produced by simple mechanisms with nothing felt at all. If animals feel little or nothing, the moral case collapses.

## Response

Part of this is right. We cannot observe another being's experience directly, and anthropomorphism is a real error. Animals will not suffer in precisely the way we do, and some species are far better evidence than others.

But the question has been studied, and prominent groups of scientists who study it have concluded the opposite. In 2012 a group of neuroscientists meeting at Cambridge [declared](https://fcmconference.org/img/CambridgeDeclarationOnConsciousness.pdf) that the neural substrates of emotion are not confined to the cortex, that affect-related systems are concentrated in subcortical regions where homologies with humans abound, and that humans "are not unique in possessing the neurological substrates that generate consciousness". The 2024 [New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness](https://sites.google.com/nyu.edu/nydeclaration/declaration) says there is "strong scientific support" for conscious experience in mammals and birds, and at least a realistic possibility of it in all vertebrates and many invertebrates.

The animals we farm in the largest numbers are birds and mammals, which is exactly where the support is strongest. 

Uncertainty at the edges does not license indifference at the centre. For a pig or a chicken the uncertainty is small, the cost to them is large, and the benefit to us is a meal we can replace.

## In short

- Leading neuroscientists have said humans are not unique in having the brain basis for consciousness.
- Support is strongest for mammals and birds, which is most of what we farm.
- Uncertainty about shrimp or insects is no reason to ignore near-certainty about pigs and hens.
