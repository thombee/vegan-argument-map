## The objection at full strength

Fish have no neocortex, the part of the human brain most associated with conscious experience. They do not vocalise, they have no facial expression we can read, and their reflexive wriggling on a line could be nociception with nothing felt. Serious scientists, like Brian Key, have argued that fish cannot feel pain, and the case deserves to be taken seriously rather than waved away.

## Response

There is real scientific disagreement, and it is fair to say so. But the weight of evidence is against the claim that fish feel nothing.

In 2003 [Sneddon and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.2003.2349) found nociceptors on the heads of rainbow trout that responded to pressure, heat above 40C and a mild acid. When noxious substances were applied to the trout's lips, their gill-beat rate rose, they took longer to resume feeding and they behaved unusually for a prolonged period. The authors read that as discomfort rather than a reflex that switches off when the stimulus ends.

The cortex argument is the strongest argument on the other side. [Sneddon and Leach](https://www.wellbeingintlstudiesrepository.org/animsent/vol1/iss3/28/) summarise Key's case: fish lack a human-like cortex. They reply that Key accepts that birds feel pain even though bird brains are structured very differently from mammalian ones, and that gaps in knowledge do not justify concluding that fish do not feel pain. The 2024 [New York Declaration](https://sites.google.com/nyu.edu/nydeclaration/declaration) takes the same line: at least a realistic possibility of conscious experience in all vertebrates, fishes included.

So the careful position is that fish pain is not as thoroughly established as mammal pain, and is well supported enough to take seriously. With very large numbers of fish at stake and a replaceable benefit to us, "probably not" would not be a good enough reason to proceed.

## In short

- Trout have nociceptors and show lasting behavioural change after harm, not just a reflex.
- The no-cortex argument would also rule out birds.
- Scientists disagree, but a realistic possibility of pain is enough to take fish seriously.
