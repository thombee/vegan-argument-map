## The objection at full strength

You have conceded that animals feel something, but the strength of an experience matters morally. A human's dread of an operation, of death, of disgrace has layers an animal lacks. A modest, momentary animal discomfort cannot weigh the same as a human interest, and a meal is a human interest.

## Response

There is probably something right in this. Humans can suffer in ways that depend on language and anticipation, and a view that treats every harm as identical is not a view anyone holds, including most vegans.

But it is a leap from "different" to "much less". Physical pain, fear, frustration and distress do not depend on language. An animal in chronic distress has no need of abstract thought to be miserable. And in some cases an animal may suffer more than we would, because it cannot understand why it is being handled or that it will end.

Even granting a heavy discount, the sums do not come out in favour of the practice. The [Stanford Encyclopedia](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/moral-animal/) describes the standard way of weighing this: rank interests by how crucial they are. An animal's interest in not suffering or dying is serious; a person's interest in eating that animal when other food is available is replaceable. A discounted serious interest still outweighs a replaceable one.

The honest conclusion is that animal suffering counts for less than a comparable human harm in some cases, and still enough that a taste preference does not justify it.

## In short

- Animals may suffer differently from us; that does not make it small.
- Pain, fear and distress do not need language.
- A discounted serious interest still outweighs a replaceable one such as a meal.
