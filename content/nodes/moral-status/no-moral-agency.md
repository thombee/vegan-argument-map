## The objection at full strength

Rights belong to a system of mutual obligation. People respect each other's rights because everyone can be held to account. A lion does not respect a gazelle's right to life, and a chicken cannot be wronged by us because it cannot wrong us. Rights are a human institution, and it makes no sense to extend them outside it.

## Response

There is a coherent tradition behind this, and it is right that rights in the legal sense are human institutions. But the link between having duties and being owed something fails in ordinary cases.

An infant has no duties. Neither does a person in a coma or with a severe cognitive disability. We hold, without much hesitation, that each of them can be wronged and that we owe them things. So being a moral agent, someone who can bear duties, cannot be a requirement for being a moral patient, someone to whom duties are owed.

The [Stanford Encyclopedia](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/moral-animal/) lays out the leading alternatives: that sentience is what grounds moral standing, or that being a subject of a life with a welfare of its own does. On these views, what matters is that the being can be harmed and can fare well or badly.

You do not have to believe in "animal rights" in a legal sense to accept the conclusion. If it is wrong to cause serious suffering for trivial reasons, that applies whatever the sufferer can do in return.

## In short

- Infants and some adults have no duties but are owed protection.
- Being owed something depends on being harmable, not on being able to reciprocate.
- You can accept the conclusion without any theory of rights.
