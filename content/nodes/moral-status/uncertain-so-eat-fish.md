## The objection at full strength

Fine, you cannot be sure fish suffer, but you cannot be sure they do not either. In ordinary life we act on uncertain knowledge. Until the science is settled, nobody can be blamed for carrying on as normal.

## Response

It is true that the question is not closed, and I would rather say so than overclaim. But the move from "unsettled" to "carry on" is not neutral. It treats the possibility that fish suffer as if it were zero.

The [New York Declaration](https://sites.google.com/nyu.edu/nydeclaration/declaration) says the evidence indicates at least a realistic possibility of conscious experience in all vertebrates, fishes among them. The [Stanford Encyclopedia](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/moral-animal/) records that several authors recommend erring on the side of caution where sentience is uncertain.

Ordinary decisions work this way too. If there is a realistic chance a building is occupied, you do not demolish it because nobody has proven anyone is inside. The size of the possible harm and the cost of avoiding it both matter, and here the cost of avoiding it is a different dinner.

Waiting for certainty can also be a way of never deciding. If you think the evidence will eventually settle, a sensible response is to eat less fish now and update later.

## In short

- A realistic possibility of suffering is not zero.
- Where sentience is uncertain, philosophers advise caution.
- The cost of caution is a different meal.
