## The objection at full strength

If sentience is the criterion and bivalves lack a central brain, the consistent position is that eating them is permitted. A reasonable vegan should accept that, and if not, the view looks like a dogma about animals rather than a principle about suffering.

## Response

There is a real argument here and some vegans accept it. If the commitment is to avoid causing suffering, and bivalves are very unlikely to suffer, then eating them harms nothing in particular.

The difficulty is how well we know that. The [Wageningen report](https://edepot.wur.nl/577988) found no papers directly showing whether bivalves are sentient, and noted a few studies on predator avoidance, learning and serotonin which suggest they might be. It is not evidence they suffer; it is a reminder that "no evidence" and "evidence of absence" are different.

So my answer is the honest one: it is defensible either way, and the veganism is not undermined by it. Those who want to eat bivalves on principle can argue for it within the framework.

What the argument does not do is rescue pork or chicken. Whether or not an oyster is permitted, a pig, which belongs to a group where support for conscious experience is strong, is not.

## In short

- Bivalves are the most arguable case, and some ethical vegans eat them.
- The evidence is absent, not negative.
- Whichever way it falls, it doesn't license eating pigs or hens.
