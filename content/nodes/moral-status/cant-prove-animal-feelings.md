## The objection at full strength

Even if the brain evidence is suggestive, it is only correlation. Nobody can enter a pig's head. You are asking people to change their lives on the basis of an inference, and inferences can be wrong.

## Response

That is correct: we cannot prove it, and the [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/moral-animal/) notes that sentience is a non-observable capacity, so there is "a lot of uncertainty" about distantly related animals. Honest vegans should say so.

But the standard of proof is being set unevenly. We cannot prove the minds of other people either. We infer them from behaviour, from similar bodies, and from shared origins. Every one of those grounds applies to a pig or a hen, only to a lesser degree than to a chimp, and far more than to an oyster.

The philosophical literature draws a practical conclusion: where sentience is uncertain, the entry says several authors recommend erring "on the side of caution". Caution costs very little here. What is on the other side of the scale is a choice about dinner that most of us can make differently.

So the argument is not "it is proven, therefore stop". It is "the evidence strongly favours experience, the stakes for the animal are severe, and the benefit to us is replaceable".

## In short

- No one can prove any mind but their own; we act on the best inference.
- Where sentience is uncertain, philosophers recommend erring on the side of caution.
- For farm animals the inference is strong and the cost of caution is low.
