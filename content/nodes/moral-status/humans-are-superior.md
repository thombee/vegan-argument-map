## The objection at full strength

Humans can reason, plan, build civilisations, and reflect on their own deaths. Nothing that a pig does remotely compares. It is natural to think such differences give humans higher moral status, and most moral traditions agree. This is a mainstream view, and animals matter some, but not as much as people.

## Response

There is truth in the observation: humans have capacities no other animal has, and those capacities matter for some things. A human can be wronged in ways a chicken cannot, for example by being lied to or deprived of a career.

The leap is from "more capable in some ways" to "so their interests can be set aside". Bentham addressed this [in a footnote](https://www.econlib.org/library/Bentham/bnthPML18.html): a full-grown horse or dog is more rational and conversable than a newborn infant, and he concludes that the question is not "Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?" We do not say a baby or a person with severe cognitive disability matters less in the sense that we can use them for food. Whatever grounds their claim, it cannot be intelligence.

The [Stanford Encyclopedia](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/moral-animal/) calls this the argument from marginal cases and notes that it creates serious difficulties for personhood as the criterion of moral considerability. People respond in different ways, but the underlying point remains: if intelligence is the measure, some humans fall below some animals.

Equal consideration does not mean identical treatment. It means a pig's pain counts the same as a human's pain of the same intensity. That is enough to rule out most of what is done to them.

## In short

- Capacities differ; the capacity to suffer is what gives interests weight.
- Infants and some disabled adults are less capable than many animals yet matter fully.
- Equal consideration is not identical treatment.
