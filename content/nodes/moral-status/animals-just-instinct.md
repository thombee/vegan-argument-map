## The objection at full strength

Animals behave in ways that look like distress, but evolution can build an animal that flinches, cries out and avoids danger without anything being felt. A thermostat reacts to heat without feeling hot. Why assume the animal is more than a very good thermostat?

## Response

The thermostat comparison is a fair way to put the worry, and it is why scientists distinguish reflex (nociception) from felt pain. But "instinct" does not settle the matter, because humans run on instinct too. Our flinch, our fear and our hunger are inherited and unlearned, and we feel every one of them.

The [Cambridge Declaration](https://fcmconference.org/img/CambridgeDeclarationOnConsciousness.pdf) notes that artificially stimulating the same subcortical brain regions in animals and in humans produces corresponding behaviour and feeling states, and that many of the resulting behaviours "are consistent with experienced feeling states", including states that are rewarding and punishing. Where the brain machinery is the same and the behaviour is the same, the simplest explanation is that the experience is the same kind of thing.

Also, "it is just instinct" proves too much. If an inherited, automatic response meant nothing was felt, it would apply to a newborn baby's cry, which we do not treat as empty.

Nobody needs certainty to act decently. We act on the best explanation of the evidence everywhere else in life, including when a dog yelps.

## In short

- Instinct and felt experience are not alternatives; humans have both.
- Matching brain circuits plus matching behaviour points to matching experience.
- The same argument would dismiss a newborn's cry.
