## The objection at full strength

Cruelty to animals is wrong, but only because it damages the character of the person who does it or harms someone's property. That lets us condemn dog-fighting without conceding that dogs themselves are owed anything.

## Response

This is the view of Kant and a long tradition, and it has the merit of agreeing that cruelty is wrong. The [Stanford Encyclopedia](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/moral-animal/) describes it as treating animals as indirectly considerable.

The weakness shows up when you ask what is wrong in a case where no human is affected: a stray animal tormented where no one sees, with no one's property or character in question. Almost everyone thinks the wrongness is in what happens to the animal. The animal's pain is the reason, not a side effect of something else.

And if the animal's pain is the reason we condemn the cruelty, then the same reason is present when the pain is caused for food. The indirect view would need to say why a human's habit of eating animals does not corrupt, while a habit of tormenting them does.

So the view agrees with the conclusion in the clearest cases and then cannot hold the line when the harm is routine and institutional.

## In short

- The indirect view explains cruelty by what it does to humans.
- It struggles with cases where no human is affected.
- If the animal's pain is the reason, it applies to farming too.
