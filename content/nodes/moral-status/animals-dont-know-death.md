## The objection at full strength

A person killed painlessly loses a future they were planning. A cow has no plans and no concept of dying. If nothing is felt and nothing is anticipated, then nobody is harmed by an instant death.

## Response

I will concede the strongest part. The [Stanford Encyclopedia](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/moral-animal/) says in its discussion of the seal example that it is "less clear" that the animal has a future-directed interest in continued existence, one that persons arguably have and non-persons arguably do not. Some philosophers, including some who take animals very seriously, think painless killing is therefore less bad for animals than for people.

Three things still follow. First, less bad is not nothing. The animal is a being whose life goes well or badly for it, and the entry notes that on one view life appears among the central capabilities for flourishing. Second, a being does not need to understand death to be harmed by it: a baby cannot, and we do not think killing a baby harms no one. Third, even on the most favourable assumption the weighing remains. The entry's framework ranks the animal's interests against a replaceable human one, and eating it is replaceable when other food is available.

So the best that can be said is that painless killing is less bad than painful killing, which is true, and which still leaves it needing a justification that a meal does not provide.

## In short

- It is less clear that animals have a stake in continued life, and that should be admitted.
- A baby doesn't understand death either, yet killing one is a serious wrong.
- A lesser harm still needs a justification, and taste is not one.
