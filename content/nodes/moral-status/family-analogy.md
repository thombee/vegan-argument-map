## The objection at full strength

You accept that I owe more to my children than to strangers. That is partiality, and nobody calls it a vice. Species loyalty is the same structure, with humanity as the family.

## Response

The structures are similar, but they differ where it matters. Special obligations to family concern whom I must help first. They do not typically permit me to harm a stranger for my own convenience. I may save my child before yours, but I may not take your child's food to give mine a treat.

Eating animals is the second kind of case. It is not a matter of prioritising whom to help; it is inflicting harm on a third party for a preference. The [Stanford Encyclopedia](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/moral-animal/) discussion of speciesism turns on whether species membership can carry moral weight in itself: if the animal can be harmed, why is the harm excused by the identity of the beneficiary?

So I accept the family analogy, and it supports a limited conclusion: humans can reasonably come first in a rescue or a hard trade-off. It does not support the conclusion that animals can be harmed for taste when the alternative costs us little.

## In short

- Special obligations decide whom to help first, not whom we may harm.
- Eating animals is causing harm for a preference.
- Partiality supports priority in hard cases, not permission for routine harm.
