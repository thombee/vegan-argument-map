## The objection at full strength

The soy moratorium protects the Amazon, but soy moved to the Cerrado savannah, which is also a biodiversity hotspot. Soy can also displace cattle, which then move into forest. Your 'it is mostly pasture' answer ignores how connected the two are.

## Response

Both points are fair, and [Our World in Data](https://ourworldindata.org/soy) makes them. It reports that in 2015 only 13% of soy expansion came from the Amazon while 48% came from the Cerrado, and that indirect effects are harder to quantify, with studies that look only at direct conversion probably underestimating soy's role.

That is a reason to want less soy grown, not a reason to want more animal farming. Whatever the displacement effects, the bulk of the soy crop, about 77%, exists to feed animals. A diet without animal products needs far less soy than a diet with them, because feeding it through an animal is the inefficient path: beef returns only [about 2% of the energy fed to cattle](https://ourworldindata.org/land-use-diets).

If you want to be careful, look for tofu and soy milk whose suppliers certify that their soy is deforestation-free. That is a more useful signal than avoiding soy altogether.

There remains a larger point. Land is limited, and any food system that wants to protect the Cerrado and the Amazon needs to cut the amount of land used per meal. Plants eaten directly do that better than plants converted into meat.

## In short

- Soy expansion in the Cerrado and indirect land-use effects are real.
- Most of that soy is feed, so cutting animal products reduces the pressure.
- Deforestation-free soy for tofu and soy milk is the sensible refinement.
