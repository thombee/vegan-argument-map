## The objection at full strength

The parent response says the effect is small overall. But individual farms publish soil tests showing carbon rising year on year, and the reviews themselves admit that in some places the gain can offset the whole herd's emissions. If one farm can be carbon neutral or better, the claim that beef is bad for the climate is not true of that farm.

## Response

This is partly correct, and the research says so. [Godde et al. (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10584-020-02673-x) accept that under specific agro-ecological conditions, soil carbon sequestration can partly or entirely offset the emissions of a grazing system. A farm doing that is doing something worthwhile for its soil.

The problem is what the offset can and cannot do. The authors describe the effect as time-limited: it does not go on indefinitely, while the herd's emissions do. They also describe it as reversible, meaning carbon stored in the soil can be lost again. Emissions from the animals, by contrast, are ongoing. The authors conclude that at global level the emissions of grazing systems outweigh the sequestration, which is also the finding of the [Food Climate Research Network report](https://www.tabledebates.org/publication/grazed-and-confused).

There is a scale problem too. The offset happens on a particular farm, with a particular soil and rainfall, and it cannot be multiplied across the global beef herd. Even if every pasture performed as well as the best, you would still be asking land to do two jobs: feed cattle and store carbon, when it could do the second job without the first.

So the honest summary is that a few farms are doing something real, and that it does not make beef a climate-positive food for the people who eat it.

## In short

- Some grazing systems can offset part or all of their emissions, for a while and in the right conditions.
- The gain saturates and can be reversed; the animals' emissions are ongoing.
- It does not scale to the global herd.
