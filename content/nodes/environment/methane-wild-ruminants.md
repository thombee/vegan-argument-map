## The objection at full strength

Ruminants have been belching methane for millions of years. If farmed cattle replace wild herds, the total might be no higher than it used to be. It makes no sense to blame the cow for something the wild equivalents always did.

## Response

Wild grazers did emit methane, and the climate was in balance with it. The relevant point is that the baseline included them, and that human-caused emissions are measured on top of that baseline.

The UN assessment, as [reported by Down To Earth](https://www.downtoearth.org.in/agriculture/reduce-human-caused-methane-emissions-by-45-to-avoid-worst-of-climate-change-un-76837), counts manure and enteric fermentation from livestock as about 32% of human-caused methane. Those emissions come from a farmed herd that is raised to meet human demand. What matters for warming is the extra methane in the atmosphere compared with a world without that farming, and methane has been responsible for about 30% of warming since pre-industrial times.

The principle holds whatever the exact numbers of wild and farmed animals: whether a source is "natural" does not decide its effect on the climate. If we reduced farmed herds and let ecosystems recover, wild grazers could fill part of the gap, and that would be a fair thing to want. It is not an argument for keeping the current herd.

The appeal to nature also sits uneasily with the ethical question. See [it's natural](/a/its-natural/) for why.

## In short

- Wild ruminants were part of the pre-industrial baseline; farmed herds are added on top.
- Livestock account for roughly 32% of human-caused methane.
- Natural origin does not settle the climate effect, or the ethics.
