## The objection at full strength

Restoration sounds easy on a spreadsheet. In practice, land that has been grazed for a century may be degraded, weedy or exposed to fire. Who pays, who manages it, and what happens to the rural communities? Cattle are working landscapes. Rewilding is a hope.

## Response

It is true that restoration is not automatic, and that the numbers are modelled estimates. [Hayek et al.](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41893-020-00603-4) say as much. Their figure of 152.5 gigatons of carbon in living plant biomass is a potential, and they describe the additional soil and litter carbon as carrying "large but unknown uncertainty".

Three points matter, though. Nobody has to restore every hectare for the argument to work. Even partial restoration of the lowest-value pasture would store meaningful carbon. Second, the case against relying on cattle does not depend on rewilding; it only needs the land to not be required for meat. A smaller herd on the best grazing land would be a very different system from today's. And third, the transition for people is a real issue that deserves real support. It is dealt with on the [farmers' livelihoods](/a/farmers-jobs/) page.

The direction of the evidence is that ending the demand for beef frees land, and that some of that land is more useful to the climate and to wildlife in its natural state. How fast and where are fair open questions.

## In short

- Restoration estimates are modelled potentials with real uncertainty.
- The argument needs only some land to be freed, not every hectare restored.
- The human transition for farmers deserves support and is a separate question.
