## The objection at full strength

Regenerative graziers argue that cattle, moved often across pasture, stimulate grass growth and build soil carbon. On this view a well-run beef farm is not part of the climate problem but part of the fix: the grass pulls carbon out of the air, the roots lock it in the soil, and the cattle are the tool that makes it happen. It is paired with a fair point about industrial beef, where feedlots, imported feed and cleared forest do real damage.

Some farmers measure real gains in soil carbon on their own land, and some researchers back the idea in specific settings. The question is whether that scales into a reason to eat beef.

## Response

The most thorough review of this question is the Food Climate Research Network's [Grazed and Confused?](https://www.tabledebates.org/publication/grazed-and-confused). It accepts that good grazing management can raise soil carbon in some places. It then finds that the gain is small, temporary and reversible, and that it is "substantially outweighed" by the greenhouse gases the animals themselves produce. Its conclusion is that grass-fed beef is not a meaningful climate solution. A later peer-reviewed paper by [Godde and colleagues (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10584-020-02673-x) says the same: under specific conditions, sequestration can partly or even entirely offset the emissions of a particular grazing system, but any sequestration is time-limited, reversible, and at global level outweighed by grazing emissions. They still support building soil carbon where it also helps the farm, especially on degraded land.

Two words in that finding matter. The gain is time-limited, while the animals keep emitting year after year. And it is reversible: carbon stored in soil can be released again.

There is also the comparison that matters. Poore and Nemecek's [global analysis of 38,700 farms](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aaq0216) found that the impacts of the lowest-impact animal products typically exceed those of vegetable substitutes. Better beef is better than worse beef, but it is not better than lentils, tofu or nuts.

None of this is an argument against restoring grassland. It is an argument against treating a steak as a carbon-positive purchase. And whether the animal lived on grass or grain, the ethical question of whether we should kill it for a meal is untouched by the soil chemistry.

## In short

- Review evidence says grazing carbon gains are limited, reversible and outweighed by the animals' emissions.
- Better-managed beef is lower impact than worse beef, but still higher than most plant foods.
- Soil carbon is a reason to manage land well, not a reason to eat more beef.
