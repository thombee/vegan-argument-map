## The objection at full strength

Grassland is a legitimate ecosystem, and many grasslands evolved with large grazing animals. Removing livestock does not return the land to a pristine state; it may just become scrub or a fire hazard. Cattle at least turn it into food and keep the farm going.

## Response

Some grassland should stay grassland, and nothing here argues for planting trees on a prairie. But much pasture, especially in the tropics and in wetter regions, would regrow forest or woodland if left alone.

[Hayek et al. (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41893-020-00603-4) mapped this. They estimate that pastures for ruminant meat and dairy account for 72% of the "carbon opportunity cost" of animal agriculture, meaning the carbon native vegetation would take up if it were allowed to regrow. They calculate that a shift to plant-based diets by 2050 could allow 332 to 547 GtCO2 to be sequestered, equivalent to 99 to 163% of the CO2 budget for a 66% chance of limiting warming to 1.5 degrees. They are open that the soil and litter part of the estimate carries large uncertainty.

Our World in Data's [land-use analysis](https://ourworldindata.org/land-use-diets) puts the scale in context: a global plant-based diet would cut agricultural land from 4.1 billion to about 1 billion hectares. That is a huge area of potential habitat and carbon store.

So the choice is not between grazing and nothing. It is between grazing and a restored ecosystem, with the second often storing more carbon and supporting more wildlife than the first. People can reasonably debate which land should be restored and which left as managed grassland. That is a decision about particular places, and it is not a reason to expect everyone to keep eating beef.

## In short

- Freed pasture often has a better use: regrowing native vegetation that holds more carbon.
- Estimates are large but uncertain, and some grassland should stay grassland.
- Which land to restore is a local decision, not a reason for everyone to keep eating beef.
