## The objection at full strength

Individual plant foods are low in one or more essential amino acids: grains are low in lysine, legumes in methionine. Animal protein contains all of them in good proportions. So, the argument goes, a vegan has to be constantly combining foods to avoid a shortfall, and may fall short anyway.

## Response

The premise is correct for single foods, and it is the reason variety matters. Where the argument fails is in treating it as a practical problem for people who eat ordinary meals. The [review by Mariotti and Gardner](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu11112661) examined actual protein and amino acid intakes of vegetarian and vegan adults and concluded that protein-rich plant foods, "traditional legumes, nuts and seeds", are sufficient to reach full protein adequacy, while the question of any amino acid deficiency has been "substantially overstated".

You do not need to combine foods at each meal. Eating a mix of legumes, soy foods, grains, nuts and seeds across the day is enough, which is what the [Dietitians Australia vegan guide](https://member.dietitiansaustralia.org.au/Common/Uploaded%20files/DAA/Resource_Library/2020/VF_A_Guide_to_Vegan_Eating.pdf) lists as protein sources: legumes, soy foods such as tofu and tempeh, textured vegetable protein, nuts, seeds and wholegrains. Soy foods are particularly handy, but they are not the only way to meet your needs.

Honest caveats: older adults are described in that review as a more complex case, and anyone eating very little food overall, as in some restrictive or very low-energy patterns, can fall short of protein on any diet. If you are worried, a dietitian can review your intake.

## In short

- The 'incomplete protein' point is true of single foods and misleading for whole diets.
- A variety of legumes, grains, nuts, seeds and soy foods over a day covers the essential amino acids.
- No need to match foods at each meal.
