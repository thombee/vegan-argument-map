## The objection at full strength

A systematic review of the research on plant-based children found that vegan children can grow less than omnivorous children and have lower bone mineral content. That is exactly what you would fear if a diet is short of calcium, energy or protein, and it should settle the question for a parent.

## Response

The finding is real and should be taken seriously. The [2024 systematic review by Desmond, Fewtrell and Wells](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu16050723) concluded that vegan, but not vegetarian, diets can restrict growth relative to omnivorous children and raise the risk of being stunted or underweight, although the percentage affected is relatively small. It also found lower bone mineral content in vegetarian and especially vegan children, B12 deficiency in children not supplemented (which supplementation corrects), lower vitamin D and iron stores if unsupplemented (though iron markers were usually normal), and a risk of iodine deficiency that might affect the thyroid. Vegan children had a more favourable lipid profile.

Take that as a map of what to manage. B12, vitamin D and iodine can be supplemented or planned, and growth is something paediatric teams can measure. What the review calls for is better recommendations and safety measures, and its authors note that national guidance is mixed: some countries support plant-based diets in infants, children and adolescents, others discourage them.

So the reasonable position is the one the studies support: if a family raises a child on a vegan diet, they need to supplement B12, plan the rest with a dietitian, and watch growth, rather than assume it will take care of itself.

## In short

- Vegan children can show restricted growth and lower bone mineral content in the research; the share affected is small.
- B12, vitamin D and iodine deficits are preventable with supplements and planning.
- Growth should be monitored by a doctor or dietitian.
