## The objection at full strength

Even if plant proteins can supply every amino acid, they are digested and used somewhat less efficiently, and lab studies suggest animal protein stimulates muscle protein synthesis more. Add to that a meta-analysis which found animal protein did better for lean mass in younger adults, and it looks like a real, measurable disadvantage for anyone who cares about muscle.

## Response

There is a real difference in the lab, and the trials do show something. The [meta-analysis by Lim and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu13020661) pooled randomised trials and found that protein source did not change absolute lean mass or muscle strength overall. In younger adults, however, animal protein produced a small extra gain in lean mass (about 0.4 kg). That is a genuine finding and we should not wave it away.

It is also a modest one, and it does not show that plant-based eaters cannot build muscle. In a [12-week resistance training trial](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01434-9), 19 habitual vegan men and 19 omnivores had their daily protein topped up to the same level (1.6 g per kg of body weight), with soy for the vegans and whey for the omnivores. Both groups gained lean mass and muscle size, with no significant difference between them. That study is small, short and in young men, so it cannot settle every question, but it is the kind of direct test that matters.

The practical reading: if you are training hard and eating plants, aim for enough total protein and consider a bit more of it. That is a tweak, not a barrier. See also [Can vegans build muscle and perform as athletes?](/a/athletes-strength/).

## In short

- Pooled trials found no overall difference in strength or total lean mass by protein source.
- Younger adults gained about 0.4 kg more lean mass with animal protein: small but real.
- In a matched-protein trial, vegans and omnivores gained the same.
