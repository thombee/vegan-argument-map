## The objection at full strength

Isoflavones act like weak oestrogens, and breast cancers often grow in response to oestrogen. Studies in mice show that isoflavones can make oestrogen-sensitive tumours grow. If you have had breast cancer, or are at risk, that seems like a good reason to avoid soy.

## Response

The animal finding is real, which is why the worry persists. A [2025 narrative review](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13668-025-00640-5) notes that isoflavones stimulated existing oestrogen-sensitive mammary tumours in a particular mouse model (athymic, ovariectomised mice). But it concludes that extensive clinical research shows neither soy foods nor isolated isoflavones affect markers of breast cancer risk such as mammographic density and breast cell proliferation, even at exposures well above typical Asian intakes.

On outcomes, the review reports that observational data indicate soy intake after diagnosis is linked to lower recurrence and possibly lower mortality, and that limited data show no interference with tamoxifen or aromatase inhibitors. It concludes that women with breast cancer can safely eat soy foods and suggests keeping to about two servings of traditional soy foods a day.

Caveats we should state plainly: this is a narrative review, much of the outcome evidence is observational, and it is not a substitute for advice from an oncologist. If you are being treated for breast cancer, discuss your diet with your treating team. What the evidence does not support is the claim that eating soy causes breast cancer.

## In short

- Mouse studies are the origin of the worry; human clinical evidence does not show harm.
- A 2025 review found no effect on breast cancer risk markers and links postdiagnosis soy intake to lower recurrence.
- If you are in treatment, ask your oncology team.
