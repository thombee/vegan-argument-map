## The objection at full strength

The studies you cite recruit recreational runners and gym-goers for a few months. Elite performance depends on small margins, years of training and recovery, and every nutrient. You cannot generalise from a 12-week trial in 38 students to a Tour de France rider.

## Response

That is a fair limitation. The [resistance-training trial](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01434-9) had 19 vegans and 19 omnivores over 12 weeks, and the [systematic review](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijsnem.2015-0231) could identify only eight qualifying studies. Nobody should claim these results settle elite performance, and we do not. What they support is a narrower point: the common assertion that a vegan diet makes athletic performance impossible or clearly worse is not borne out in the controlled data that exist.

For elite athletes, small gaps in energy, iron, B12 or protein matter more, and it makes sense to work with a sports dietitian and check bloods. That applies to every diet. We would also say the decision to eat vegan is mainly ethical, so an athlete who thinks it costs them performance can reasonably ask for individual advice rather than give up on the diet.

## In short

- The studies are small and recreational; they cannot speak to elite sport.
- They do contradict the claim that a vegan diet makes performance impossible.
- Elite athletes should use a sports dietitian and regular blood tests.
