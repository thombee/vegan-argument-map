## The objection at full strength

I'm blood type O, and the blood type diet says I should eat meat. Everyone is different, and some people just seem to need animal protein. More broadly, genes affect how we absorb iron and B12, convert plant fats, and respond to carbohydrates, so what works for one body fails in another.

## Response

Individual differences exist, but the blood-type diet is the one claim here that has been tested. In the Toronto Nutrigenomics study of [1,455 adults](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0084749) people who followed the diet for their own blood type did no better than those who followed a different one. Adherence to some of the "blood-type" diets was associated with better cardiometabolic markers, but those associations did not depend on the person's ABO genotype, so the authors concluded the findings did not support the hypothesis. A second analysis of [973 overweight adults](https://doi.org/10.1093/jn/nxx074) found the same: matching a diet to genotype did not change the effect, "suggesting that the theory behind this diet is not valid".

The broader point, that people differ, is right, and it is the reason dietitians individualise advice. We cannot assess anyone's genetics from a website, and it would be wrong to claim every body responds the same way. Some people have medical conditions that call for tailored advice, and [Dietitians Australia](https://member.dietitiansaustralia.org.au/Common/Uploaded%20files/DAA/Resource_Library/2020/VF_A_Guide_to_Vegan_Eating.pdf) recommends checking with a dietitian or doctor about supplements.

What the evidence does not support is a rule that certain people, by blood type or ancestry, must eat meat. If you feel you do, a blood test and a dietitian are better guides than a diet book.

## In short

- The blood-type diet theory was tested in two studies and not supported.
- People do differ, which is why advice should be individual.
- A doctor or dietitian is a better guide than a blood-type book.
