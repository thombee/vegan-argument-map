## The objection at full strength

Vegetarians and vegans in these cohorts exercise more, smoke less and eat more fibre for reasons unrelated to meat. Health-conscious people choose vegan diets, so the studies tell you about the people, not the food.

## Response

This is a valid methodological point, and good researchers make it themselves. The [meta-analysis by Dinu and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1080/10408398.2016.1138447) pools observational studies, and the German Nutrition Society's [2024 review](https://www.pharmazeutische-zeitung.de/gesund-und-gruen-148427/) rates the certainty of much of the evidence as low to very low. The authors of EPIC-Oxford, [Tong and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.l4897), adjusted for sociodemographic and lifestyle factors, which reduces but cannot remove the problem of unmeasured differences.

That is why we avoid strong claims. We do not say a vegan diet prevents or cures disease. We say that dietetic bodies conclude a planned vegan diet is adequate, that the observational data on adults show a mixed picture, and that some measured outcomes, like fractures, are worse. If you doubt the benefits, the argument still leaves the central position intact: the case against eating animals does not rest on vegans being healthier.

## In short

- Observational studies cannot prove cause: vegans differ in other ways.
- Reviews rate much of the evidence as low certainty, which is why the claim here is 'adequate', not 'miraculous'.
- The ethical case does not depend on health benefits.
